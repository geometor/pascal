People¶

Biographical register of geometers, mathematicians, and historical figures associated with Blaise Pascal’s Essay pour les Coniques (1640) and the projective evolution of the Hexagrammum Mysticum.

Blaise Pascal¶

(1623–1662 · France)

French mathematician, physicist, and religious philosopher. At age sixteen, Pascal presented to the Paris academy of Father Marin Mersenne a single printed broadsheet—Essay pour les coniques (February 1640)—announcing the Hexagrammum Mysticum (Pascal’s Theorem): opposite sides of any hexagon inscribed in a conic section intersect in three collinear points.

Girard Desargues¶

(1591–1661 · Lyon & Paris)

French geometer and architect, founder of modern synthetic projective geometry. In his 1639 Brouillon project, Desargues introduced points at infinity, pencils of lines (ordonnances), and projective involutions. Pascal explicitly acknowledged that his entire method grew from studying the writings of “M. Desargues Lyonnois”.

Pappus of Alexandria¶

(fl. c. 320–340 CE · Alexandria, Roman Egypt)

Last great synthetic geometer of antiquity, author of the eight-book Synagoge (Mathematical Collection). Pappus proved the two-line Hexagon Theorem (Synagoge VII.139) thirteen centuries before Pascal showed that the two lines could be replaced by any smooth conic section.

Apollonius of Perga¶

(c. 262–c. 190 BCE · Perga & Alexandria)

Known as “The Great Geometer”, author of the eight books of the Conics (Κωνικά). Apollonius established the classical theory of conics as sections of a cone, which Desargues and Pascal transformed through projective central projection.

Jakob Steiner¶

(1796–1863 · Switzerland & Berlin)

Master of synthetic projective geometry. In 1828, Steiner proved that the 60 Pascal lines generated by six conic points concur three-by-three in 20 Steiner points ($G$), with two points arising from each complementary bi-triangle partition of the vertices.

Julius Plücker¶

(1801–1868 · Germany)

Mathematician and physicist who revolutionized analytic and projective geometry with homogeneous coordinates and line coordinates. In 1830, Plücker proved that the 20 Steiner points lie four-by-four along 15 Plücker lines.

Thomas Penyngton Kirkman¶

(1806–1895 · Great Britain)

British mathematician and clergyman who discovered in 1850 that the 60 Pascal lines concur three-by-three in a second, distinct system of 60 Kirkman points ($K$), three of which lie on every Pascal line.

Michel Chasles¶

(1793–1880 · France)

French geometer who proved that four points on an irreducible conic possess an intrinsic projective cross-ratio $(P_1, P_2; P_3, P_4)_{\mathcal{C}}$ independent of the observer point $V$ chosen on the conic (Traité des sections coniques, 1865).

Charles Julien Brianchon¶

(1783–1864 · France)

French geometer who in 1806 discovered Brianchon’s theorem, the exact projective dual of Pascal’s theorem: the three principal diagonals of a hexagon circumscribed about a conic section concur at a single point.

Marin Mersenne¶

(1588–1648 · Paris)

Minim friar and central coordinator of European science. Mersenne’s weekly Paris academy served as the forum where young Blaise Pascal, coached by his father Étienne Pascal, first presented his projective broadside to Descartes, Desargues, and Roberval.

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz¶

(1646–1716 · Germany)

Philosopher and mathematician. In 1676, while living in Paris, Leibniz was granted access to Pascal’s unpublished complete manuscript on conics (Genera Conicorum) by the Pascal family, copying substantial portions of the text before the manuscript was permanently lost.
